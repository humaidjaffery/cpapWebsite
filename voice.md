# DreamSeal Reddit Voice Guide

## Purpose

This guide captures the voice used in DreamSeal's early Reddit posts about custom-fit CPAP masks and CPAP World. It should be used for founder-written Reddit posts, community updates, feedback requests, and early product announcements.

The goal is not to sound like a polished brand. The goal is to sound like a real person who became genuinely interested in a frustrating problem, has put serious work into trying to solve it, and wants CPAP users to shape what gets built next.

## Voice in one sentence

A young, earnest builder explains an ambitious CPAP project in open and conversational language, connects it to a personal experience, and asks the community for unusually broad and honest feedback.

## Core qualities

### Earnest and mission-driven

The speaker clearly cares about the problem and is comfortable stating an ambitious goal. Phrases such as "on a mission," "the perfect custom mask," and "genuinely make CPAP masks that actually fit" communicate sincere conviction rather than detached product promotion.

The ambition is usually softened with words such as "try," "hope," and "eventual goal." This keeps the speaker excited without pretending the problem has already been solved.

### Personal before commercial

Every post gives a human reason for the work before explaining the product:

- experience at a pulmonology clinic;
- a friend's dad struggling with masks;
- a parent who has used CPAP for years;
- conversations about problems that also appear in the subreddit.

The story makes the project feel discovered through real people rather than invented as a sales opportunity.

### Community-dependent

The audience is treated as a participant in the project, not a market to be persuaded. The speaker repeatedly says that the work needs the community's help, advice, ideas, or feedback. Questions are open-ended and give readers permission to mention anything, "no matter how small."

The implied relationship is: I have done a lot of work, but you have the lived experience, so I need you to tell me what would actually be useful.

### Transparent and unfinished

The speaker openly calls the work a "project," describes the current version as a "huge work in progress," and distinguishes what exists now from the "eventual goal." This honesty is an important part of the voice. A launch should feel like an invitation to help direct the next version, not a claim that the final answer has arrived.

### Excited, but not slick

Enthusiasm comes from repetition, intensifiers, ambitious scope, and exclamation points—not marketing slogans. The writing may be slightly wordy or imperfect because it sounds like someone explaining work they care about in their own words.

## Narrator and point of view

- Use first person singular when describing work personally completed: "I spent the last two months," "I started," or "I need your feedback."
- Use first person plural for the wider custom-mask mission or work done with a friend: "we decided," "we're creating," or "our mission."
- Move naturally between "I" and "we" when both are true, but keep ownership clear.
- Address readers directly as "you," "you guys," "all of you guys," or "everyone."
- Acknowledge the community's expertise through phrases such as "many of which are reflected in this sub" and "what would actually be useful to you guys."

## Tone and emotional range

The dominant emotional sequence is:

1. Friendly opening.
2. Personal concern or disbelief about a real CPAP problem.
3. Excitement about what has been built or might be possible.
4. Humility about what is missing or uncertain.
5. Gratitude and a direct request for help.

The speaker can say that something is "crazy," "huge," or "extremely helpful," but should not sound angry, cynical, sarcastic, clinical, or superior. Frustration is aimed at the problem—poor fit, scattered information, generic sizing—not at CPAP users, clinicians, manufacturers, or the subreddit.

## Word choice

### Characteristic vocabulary

Use plain, familiar words even when describing technical work. Characteristic choices include:

- "Hey guys" or "Hey everyone"
- "For some context" or "Quick background"
- "I started getting more interested in..."
- "I always thought it was crazy that..."
- "We started working on..."
- "This is why..."
- "basically"
- "really"
- "exactly"
- "specifically"
- "personally"
- "completely"
- "huge problem"
- "huge work in progress"
- "eventual goal"
- "anything and everything"
- "no matter how small"
- "honest feedback"
- "extremely helpful"
- "genuinely"
- "actually"
- "if possible"
- "of course"
- "link in comments"

### Technical language

Technical details should be translated immediately into what they mean for a user. For example:

- "facial scanning and medical-grade 3D printing" becomes a way to make a mask fit a person's unique anatomy;
- "review analysis" becomes a way to understand leaks, comfort, fit, or pain points;
- a large dataset is described with concrete counts, followed by the practical reason it matters.

Use numbers when they demonstrate real effort or scope, but never let the post read like a research paper. The data supports the story; it is not the story.

### Intensifiers

The voice naturally uses strong emphasis: "really," "very," "huge," "completely," and occasionally repeated words such as "extremely extremely helpful." Use this sparingly enough that the most important ask still stands out. One or two strong intensifiers per paragraph is usually enough.

## Sentence style and rhythm

- Paragraphs are short, but individual sentences are often long and connected with "and," "but," "which," or "so."
- Sentences frequently begin conversationally: "But we need your advice," "This is why," "It was then," or "Also."
- Ideas build through cause and effect: personal exposure led to the mask project; the mask project led to review research; the research led to CPAP World; the unfinished site leads to the feedback request.
- Colons introduce a background story or the main question.
- Parentheses contain candid side notes or practical details: "(still a huge work in progress)," "(All for completely free, of course)," or "(Link in comments!!)."
- Semicolons are rare. Prefer commas, conjunctions, and separate paragraphs.
- Rhetorical questions are concrete and reader-centered rather than abstract.

The rhythm should feel spoken and slightly breathless in moments of enthusiasm, while remaining easy to follow.

## Grammar, capitalization, and mechanics

The original posts contain occasional typos, repeated words, loose apostrophes, and very long sentences. These imperfections contribute to a spontaneous founder voice, but new posts should not deliberately insert spelling mistakes. Preserve the conversational construction and avoid polishing it into corporate copy.

Additional conventions:

- Use contractions naturally: "I'm," "we're," "didn't," and "wasn't."
- Capitalization may be used for a single important word such as "YOUR" or "WHOLE," but should be rare.
- Exclamation points are most natural in the final thank-you or the link note. Avoid using them on every sentence.
- Use CPAP in uppercase.
- Do not over-format. A plain title, a greeting, and short paragraphs match the existing posts.

## Titles

Titles are long, specific, and written like the beginning of the story. They usually combine three elements:

1. The work or unusual effort: "I spent the last two months..."
2. The intended benefit: "trying to make it easier to understand CPAP..."
3. The need for community help: "but now I need YOUR help..."

Other established title forms are direct questions such as "What do you want in a custom mask?" and "What Would Your Perfect CPAP Mask Look Like?"

For launches, prefer a concrete, human title over a branded announcement. "I analyzed thousands of mask reviews..." fits the voice better than "Introducing CPAP World: The Definitive CPAP Platform."

## Typical post structure

### 1. Human opening

Start with "Hey guys" or "Hey everyone," followed quickly by what has been worked on or why it matters.

### 2. Personal origin

Give the shortest version of the clinic, friend, or family connection needed to explain why this speaker cared enough to begin.

### 3. Problem discovery

Describe the contradiction in plain language. Examples: a device worn for a third of someone's life comes in generic sizes, or information for a highly personal decision is scattered and impersonal.

### 4. What was built

Explain the work concretely and connect every feature to a user problem. Mention the scale of the review analysis only when the current number is verified.

### 5. Current limits and eventual goal

Say plainly that the site is a huge work in progress. Briefly separate what is available now from what the speaker hopes to add later.

### 6. Broad feedback request

Ask what readers liked, did not like, found confusing, did not trust, or wished existed. Make it clear that small comments are welcome and that criticism is genuinely useful.

### 7. Low-pressure call to action

Put the link in the comments when appropriate. End with gratitude, not a hard sell.

## How to ask for feedback

Feedback requests are the center of these posts and should be specific enough to answer while remaining open-ended. Good prompts include:

- Which parts were actually useful?
- Which parts did you not like or find confusing?
- Is there anything on a mask page you would not trust or would want explained better?
- What information do you wish you had when choosing your current mask?
- Is there anything else that could make some part of your sleep apnea or CPAP experience easier?

Follow the questions with permission to be candid: "Everything and anything would be extremely useful," "no matter how small," or "please be completely honest."

Avoid asking readers to answer a long survey's worth of questions inside one post. Two to four related prompts feel natural; a list of ten product-research questions does not.

## Calls to action

The primary call to action should be feedback. Visiting the site is the step required to give that feedback, not the emotional climax of the post.

If a waitlist is mentioned, place it after the feedback request and frame it as optional. Do not allow prizes, urgency, or sign-up language to overshadow the request for community input.

## What to avoid

- Corporate launch language: "revolutionary," "industry-leading," "ecosystem," "disrupt," or "we are thrilled to announce."
- Speaking as if the site already knows the best mask for every person.
- Unsupported claims such as "completely eliminates leaks" presented as proven results.
- Hiding limitations, unfinished sections, or uncertain data.
- Clinical authority or medical advice. The speaker is a builder learning from patient experience, not a doctor prescribing treatment.
- Defensive replies to criticism. Skepticism should be treated as useful information.
- Excessive brand repetition. Say "CPAP World" once or twice, then call it "the site," "the project," or "it."
- A polished ad-like ending. Close with the reason feedback matters and a sincere thank-you.

## Reusable writing template

**Title:** I [specific work or effort] to [practical CPAP benefit], but now I need [the community's honest feedback/help].

Hey everyone,

For some context, [personal connection to CPAP]. That was what originally made me start thinking about [specific problem].

We first started working on [custom-mask project], but while trying to [research goal], I realized [larger information problem].

This is why I spent [verified time/effort] building [name]. Right now it [plain-language description of what exists and why it may help].

It is still a huge work in progress, and [honest description of what is missing or planned]. Before I keep adding to it, I really need your guys' honest feedback: [two or three concrete questions].

Everything and anything would be extremely useful, even if it seems small. [Optional broader invitation related to the CPAP or sleep apnea experience.]

[Low-pressure link note and sincere thank-you.]

## Final editing check

Before posting, confirm that the draft:

- sounds like one person talking to the subreddit, not a company issuing a press release;
- explains the personal reason before or alongside the technical work;
- uses verified numbers and avoids claims beyond the current site;
- connects features to real CPAP frustrations;
- plainly calls the site unfinished;
- asks for criticism as clearly as it asks for praise;
- makes feedback the main call to action;
- keeps the link in the comments if required by the subreddit;
- ends with gratitude and a reason the community's response matters.
